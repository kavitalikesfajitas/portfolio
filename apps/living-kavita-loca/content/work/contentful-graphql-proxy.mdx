# Contentful GraphQL Proxy

**Role:** Technical owner — RFC, epic, multi-sprint delivery  
**Tech Stack:** TypeScript · Node.js · GraphQL · Apollo Server · Express · Redis · AWS

---

**TL;DR:** I turned a growing **REST façade over Contentful GraphQL**—the legacy **`api/content`** Lambda zoo—into a **single, honest GraphQL BFF**. The real story is the sequence: **fast Next.js POC** (first rendition **predates 2024**—**Apollo**, **graphql-tools**, and **Contentful** docs; **not** LLM- or agent-led codegen) → **hard truth about cache + load for swoosh.nike** → **RFC** → **Jira epic + tickets** → **a little over two sprints of execution** → **today: a dedicated server** (Express, **Redis**, health, CORS, telemetry) that PDP traffic can actually lean on.

## Overview

This is the kind of arc interviewers look for: **prove it fast**, **name the gap**, **write the doc**, **run the program**, **ship the production shape**.

**1 — POC (Next.js, fast).** The **first rendition** of this idea—**before 2024**—was a **Pages API** route at `pages/api/contentful/graphql` that I stood up in **short focused time** (on the order of **one strong evening to about a week**): **Apollo Server**, schema in repo, **HTTP executor** POST to Contentful, **in-memory LRU** response caching, and explicit cache-control defaults. That was still **read-the-docs engineering**: **Apollo**, **graphql-tools**, and **Contentful**—not codegen assistants or agents owning the design. Enough to show **the proxy pattern**, **real caching behavior**, and **tokens / space IDs kept server-side**—without asking the org to bet on a blank slide.

**2 — The wake-up call (load).** Pretty quickly it was obvious that **in-process caching on a Next route** was not the answer for the **traffic profile we expect on swoosh.nike**: replicas, cold paths, and **shared cache hit rate** matter as much as “it works on my laptop.” That gap is what turned a cool POC into **a platform decision**.

**3 — RFC.** I wrote and circulated the internal RFC (_Replace Content-APIs with a single GraphQL Apollo proxy for Contentful_): **context**, **anti-pattern** (`api/content`-style REST-over-GraphQL), **staged plan** (Next proof vs **dedicated hosting**), and **Contentful rate-limit** reality so caching arguments stayed grounded.

**4 — Program.** I **created the Jira epic**, **wrote the implementation tickets**, and **executed across a little over two sprints**—from the pattern we trusted to the **standalone Express** service: **`POST /contentful/graphql`**, **Redis-backed** Apollo cache for **cross-instance** hits, **`GET /contentful/health`**, CORS, optional **HTTPS** for local dev, and **CI/Docker**-friendly packaging. **One surface for content reads** instead of **N** Lambdas pretending to be REST.

The technical villain the whole time was the same: **`api/content`** looked like a tidy **OpenAPI / REST** catalog, but every route still boiled down to **hand-maintained GraphQL** and **`makeItemsRequest`**. **I drove the proxy so we would not keep paying that sprawl tax.**

## The challenge (why the old shape had to go)

- **Honesty gap** — Teams integrated like it was REST; **Contentful was always GraphQL POST**. That mismatch slowed **query evolution**, **typing**, and **reviews** because the system lied about what it was.
- **Sprawl tax** — **`api/content`** meant **one handler per concern** (`get-page`, `get-collections`, `get-product-by-slug`, …) and **more SAM surface area** whenever the CMS added a shape we had to expose.
- **Platform economics** — Every new endpoint duplicated **auth**, **caching**, **observability**, and **operational muscle memory**. The proxy collapses that into **one place to optimize** and **one place to tune TTLs** against **Contentful’s delivery limits**.
- **Cache reality** — In-process LRU is fine for a **single** Node; **Redis** is how you get **consistent** cache behavior when traffic fans out across instances.

## Before and after

**Before (`api/content`):** a widening set of **REST-shaped** Lambdas (`get-page`, `get-collections`, `get-product-by-slug`, …), each with **OpenAPI** surface area and its own **`makeItemsRequest`** + **hand-maintained GraphQL**. Clients integrated against **many URLs**; the backend still owned **many queries**—just **fragmented** across deployables.

**After (proxy BFF):** clients speak **GraphQL** to **`POST /contentful/graphql`**. The service **loads Contentful’s schema from the repo**, **wraps** it, and **forwards** operations with **server-only** credentials. **Cache policy, timeouts, and observability** are centralized instead of re-learned per Lambda.

```text
                    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
  Storefronts  ───► │  Legacy: many REST Lambdas (api/content) │
  & BFFs            │  each with its own GraphQL to Contentful │
                    └─────────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                                          ▼
                               Contentful GraphQL (CDA)

                    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
  Storefronts  ───► │  Proxy: one Apollo BFF                    │
  & BFFs            │  POST /contentful/graphql                 │
                    └─────────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                                          ▼
                               Contentful GraphQL (CDA)
```

Same vendor endpoint—**different** cost to change a query, **different** cache story under **replicas and launch traffic**.

## What I built

### Next.js POC → dedicated server

The **POC lived in the web app**; **production load on swoosh.nike** is what forced the move to a **long-lived server** and **Redis**. The **architecture is the same bet**—schema in repo, **wrap**, **delegate** to Contentful with a bearer token—just hosted where **cache, replicas, and ops** make sense.

```typescript
const executor = buildHTTPExecutor({
  endpoint: CONTENTFUL_GRAPHQL_BASE_URL,
  method: "POST",
  timeout: 5000,
  headers: { Authorization: `Bearer ${process.env.CF_DELIVERY_CONTENT_TOKEN}` },
  retry: 1,
});

const schema = wrapSchema({
  schema: contentfulSchema,
  executor,
});
```

### What that code is actually doing

- **`loadSchemaSync` + `GraphQLFileLoader`** pull a **checked-in** `contentful.schema.graphql` into a `GraphQLSchema` object. That file is the **public contract** reviewers and clients codegen against; Contentful stays an **implementation detail** behind the executor.
- **`buildHTTPExecutor`** (from `@graphql-tools/executor-http`) is the **remote transport**: every resolved field ultimately becomes a **`POST`** to `https://graphql.contentful.com/content/v1/spaces/{space}/environments/{env}` with the **CDA bearer token** in `Authorization`. **`timeout: 5000`** and **`retry: 1`** cap tail latency and give **one** bounded retry on transient failures—enough to smooth blips, not enough to **stampede** Contentful.
- **`wrapSchema`** stitches the **local SDL** to that **remote executor**: Apollo validates and plans against **your** schema file, but **execution** fans out through the HTTP executor. That is **schema delegation**, not a second graph you hand-maintain in resolvers.

### Request lifecycle (standalone server)

1. **Express** accepts **`POST /contentful/graphql`** with **`express.json({ limit: "50mb" })`** so large batched or deeply nested queries do not get rejected at the edge by default body limits.
2. **`cors`** runs on that path; preflight uses **`OPTIONS`** with **`optionsSuccessStatus: 200`** for older clients that choke on **204**.
3. **`@as-integrations/express` `expressMiddleware`** hands the HTTP request to **Apollo Server 4**.
4. Apollo **parses, validates, and executes** against the **wrapped** schema; cache plugins participate on the way out (see below).
5. The executor issues **`POST`** to Contentful’s GraphQL HTTP API and returns data **or** partial errors per GraphQL rules.

Health checks are a separate **`GET /contentful/health`** JSON response (status, message, listen URL, timestamp)—cheap **liveness** without hitting Contentful.

### Next.js POC vs Express server (same graph, different host constraints)

**POC (`pages/api/contentful/graphql`):** **`startServerAndCreateNextHandler`** from `@as-integrations/next` embeds Apollo inside a **Pages API** route. **`export const config = { maxDuration: 5 }`** caps **serverless** execution time; that alone is a hint you are not building a **long-lived** cache host. The Apollo **`cache`** was a plain **`InMemoryLRUCache`** (in the POC: **~100MB** cap, **5-minute** store TTL) shared by **parsed-query work**, **response cache**, and **cache-control**—fine for **one** warm instance, wrong shape for **many** replicas **sharing** PDP traffic.

**Production-shaped service:** **Express** + **`http` / `https.createServer`**, **`ApolloServerPluginDrainHttpServer`** for graceful shutdown, and the same **Apollo plugin stack**—but the **`cache`** is backed by **`Keyv`** and optionally **Redis** (below) so **hit rate** survives **horizontal scale**.

### Apollo plugins (why each one exists)

- **`ApolloServerPluginCacheControl`** with **`defaultMaxAge: 180`** (seconds) and **`calculateHttpHeaders: true`**. Contentful’s exported schema **does not** declare `@cacheControl` on types/fields, so without a **default**, Apollo would not emit sensible **`Cache-Control`** hints and **`@apollo/server-plugin-response-cache`** would have little to hang onto. Apollo documents this pattern for [default `maxAge` when the schema is silent](https://www.apollographql.com/docs/apollo-server/performance/caching#default-maxage).
- **`responseCachePlugin()`** stores **full successful query results** keyed by operation + cache policy. By default it **does not** cache responses that contain errors, and it aligns with the cache-control plugin so you do not cache “accidentally forever.”
- **`ApolloServerPluginLandingPageDisabled()`** in non-dev: the endpoint is **not** gated like an internal admin tool, so **Sandbox / landing** stays off.
- **`ApolloServerPluginDrainHttpServer`** on Express: clean shutdown with the Node HTTP server.
- **Telemetry:** a small **`requestDidStart` / `willSendResponse`** plugin records **`performance.now()`** duration and exports metrics (e.g. **`graphqlRequestDuration`**) with **operation name**, **route**, and **HTTP status**—the kind of dimensions you need when PDP latency regresses.

### The cache implementation (in-memory → layered → Redis)

Apollo’s **`cache`** option is not “only HTTP responses.” In both the POC and the server, the same backing store feeds **parsed/validated query caching**, **response cache**, and **cache-control** behavior—so choosing the **implementation** is choosing **PDP scalability**.

**Redis on:** the code builds a **layered** cache via **`cacheable`**: **primary** in-memory **`Keyv` + `InMemoryLRUCache`**, **secondary** **`KeyvRedis`** (TLS + `servername` in non-dev, **`disableOfflineQueue: true`** to avoid unbounded buffering when Redis is unhappy). **`KeyvAdapter`** bridges that into Apollo, with **`disableBatchReads`** wired to env for tuning **`mget`** behavior. Everything sits under a **`contentful-cache`** namespace so keys do not collide with unrelated Redis users. Optional **`DEBUG_PROXY`** hooks log **get/set** paths when you are proving behavior locally.

**Redis off:** **`Keyv`** over **`InMemoryLRUCache` only**—the POC story, explicit in logs as “Redis cache disabled.”

```typescript
// Shaped like the standalone service: layered memory + Redis, then Apollo adapter.
const layeredCache = new Cacheable({
  primary: memoryStore,
  secondary: redisStore,
  nonBlocking: true,
});
const keyValueCache = new Keyv({
  store: layeredCache,
  namespace: "contentful-cache",
});
return new KeyvAdapter(keyValueCache, { disableBatchReads });
```

### `cacheable` in practice: debugging without SSH

This part was a lot of the real engineering work.

At Nike, I could not just SSH into managed Redis infrastructure and poke around directly. So I had to prove cache behavior from the **application edge**, not from host access:

- **App-level hooks** — I instrumented `cacheable` hook points (`AFTER_SET`, `AFTER_GET`, `AFTER_GET_MANY`, `BEFORE_SECONDARY_SETS_PRIMARY`) behind a **`DEBUG_PROXY`** switch to confirm read/write flow and TTL behavior in each layer.
- **Local parity loop** — I ran local Redis via Docker and used `redis-cli monitor` while firing real GraphQL queries, then compared that output with proxy debug logs to verify key writes, read paths, and fallback behavior.
- **Synthetic request patterns** — I used repeated query runs (warm/cold mixes) to prove when responses came from L1 memory, L2 Redis, or Contentful.
- **Telemetry cross-check** — I correlated cache debug logs with request duration metrics so “it seems faster” turned into measurable p50/p95 movement.

Representative debug pattern:

```typescript
if (debugProxy) {
  layeredCache.onHook(CacheableHooks.AFTER_SET, ({ key, ttl }) => {
    console.log(`set ${key} ttl=${ttl}`);
  });
  layeredCache.onHook(CacheableHooks.AFTER_GET, ({ key, result }) => {
    console.log(`get ${key} hit=${Boolean(result?.value)}`);
  });
}
```

### Redis architecture decision (`001_redis_config.md`)

I also documented and drove the Redis decision in RFC **`001_redis_config.md`**:

- **Evaluated options** — provisioned ElastiCache replication group vs **ElastiCache Serverless**.
- **Chose serverless** — for **operational simplicity**, **automatic scaling**, **built-in failover/Multi-AZ**, and **pay-per-use** economics.
- **Explicit tradeoffs** — serverless is **not** “I own a single Redis box” and not full cluster-mode tuning either; you gain managed scaling and HA, but lose some low-level configuration knobs (for example, node shape and certain advanced controls).
- **Cost stance** — a fixed 3-node setup is predictable but can overpay during low usage; serverless aligns cost to actual usage, which matched the proxy’s variable traffic profile.

This is the key nuance: serverless cache is its own operating model. It is neither “just one instance” nor “self-managed cluster controls everywhere.” Designing around that model (and instrumenting it correctly) is what made it production-usable.

### Caching, correctness, and ops (summary)

- **Default cache age + response cache** so Contentful sees **steady, cache-friendly** traffic and clients see **stable** PDP latency.
- **Layered LRU + Redis** so **hot keys** hit memory and **shared** entries survive **process** boundaries.
- **`GET /contentful/health`**, **CORS**, optional **local HTTPS** (`mkcert` + env key paths) when the browser requires a **trusted origin** that matches **realistic** hostnames.

### Security, schema, and delivery hygiene

- **Secrets stay server-side** — The **Content Delivery API token** and **space / environment** targeting live in the **proxy process**, not in the browser bundle.
- **Schema in repo** — The Contentful schema is **materialized as GraphQL SDL in the repository** (and regenerated when space or environment changes), so **reviews** show **exactly** what clients can ask for and **codegen** can track it.
- **Production hardening** — **Introspection** and **GraphQL playground** stay **off** where the endpoint is public; **landing plugins** disabled in non-dev paths so you do not accidentally **advertise** an unauthenticated graph.
- **HTTP executor discipline** — **POST** to Contentful with a **bounded timeout** and **limited retries** so a bad release does not **pile up** hung requests against the vendor.

### Vendor reality (why caching was never optional)

Contentful’s delivery path is **rate-limited** on **uncached** hits to the **Content Delivery API**; **CDN cache hits** are the cheap path. That is why the RFC called out **vendor limits** explicitly: a proxy that **does not** define **TTLs** and **response caching** is a **foot-gun**—you either **thrash** upstream or **surprise** yourself at launch. The Apollo stack here is **intentionally opinionated** (default **max-age**, **response cache** for successful **query** operations) so behavior is **predictable** under **PDP** traffic.

### How I ran the program (not just the repo)

- **POC first** — Next.js route to **de-risk the idea** in days, not quarters.
- **RFC after the demo** — Once **cache + swoosh.nike scale** made the limit obvious, the RFC turned “we should” into **reviewable stages** and **vendor-grounded** caching strategy.
- **Planning** — **Epic + tickets** broke out **server extraction**, **Redis**, and **operational** work so execution was **trackable**.
- **Execution** — **A little over two sprints** to **today’s dedicated server**: same GraphQL story, **production** topology.

## What I’d repeat on the next platform bet

- **Write the short, blunt RFC** — It turned a recurring gripe into **funded work** with **exit criteria** stakeholders could recognize.
- **POC before the committee** — A **Next route** bought **truth** cheaply; **swoosh.nike-scale cache** forced the **honest** next step before we over-invested.
- **Name the wire protocol in the API** — When integration is GraphQL, **own it in the surface**; hiding it behind REST **taxes every future query change**.
- **Default the cache story** — Upstream schemas without hints are normal; **your server must still enforce** sane **TTLs** and **response caching** so you stay inside **delivery limits** and keep **tail latency** under control.
